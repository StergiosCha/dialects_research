## 11. Disagreements between sources on grammatical facts

The filled tables disagree on five linguistic questions that the tutor cannot leave open: what cues a geminate, whether clitic placement is categorical or variable, how negation interacts with clitics, whether the koine is a continuum or two discrete lects, and how the dialect should be spelled. They also disagree on a handful of bibliographic facts that a later verification pass has to settle. Each row names the positions, the sections where the sources sit, and the decision the tutor should take until the evidence changes.

| Topic | Position A | Position B | Decision for the tutor |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cue to plosive gemination (section 1) | Arvaniti 2001 (MGDLT1) and Christodoulou 2007 (Interspeech) treat the contrast as one of duration first, with aspiration as a secondary correlate | Armostis 2011 (PhD, Cambridge) and his ICPhS 2007 and MGDLT4 papers give aspiration and articulation a larger role in production and perception of voiceless geminates, and section 1 marks the two as disagreeing | Pronunciation feedback should score both closure duration and aspiration for ππ, ττ, κκ, and never report "too short" alone. Both sources are Ask, their recordings cannot be reused, only the stated thresholds |
| Clitic placement, categorical or variable (sections 1, 5, 7, 8) | Agouraki 2001, Terzi 1999, Condoravdi and Kiparsky 2001, Revithiadou 2006 and Chatzikyriakidis 2012 state categorical rules: enclisis in the default finite clause, proclisis under a complementizer, negation, wh element or other filled C, with the trigger inventory stated lexically or prosodically. The ΠΙ Οδηγός (section 5) teaches the same contrast as a rule (Είδες το, Εν το είδες) | Pappas 2014 (magnitude estimation), Papanicola and Armostis 2021 (corpus frequencies), Grohmann, Kambanaros, Leivada and Pavlou 2021 (free variation in adults and children), Grohmann, Pavlou, Fotiou and Leivada 2024 (one mixed grammar), Grohmann, Papadopoulou and Themistocleous 2017 (variation by gender, age, origin in 431 children) and Tsiplakou's competing grammars chapter show SMG-like proclisis inside the koine, not predicted by social factors in adults | Code the categorical rule set as the koine default and as the form the tutor produces. Accept SMG-like proclisis in learner input as acrolectal, flag it as "standard-like, not Cypriot", never as an error. Use the 2017 and 2021 rates to set tolerance thresholds per context |
| Negation and clitics (section 1) | Agouraki 2001 lists negation among the proclisis triggers | Liapis 2007 states that clitics follow the verb after έντζε but precede it after εν | The negation trigger must be lexically specific: εν (and έν) trigger proclisis, έντζε does not. Both items need a drill, and the orthography module must keep εν and έντζε apart |
| Acquisition order of clitic placement (sections 1, 8) | Neokleous 2015 finds misplacement in proclisis contexts up to about age three, so enclisis is the child's default and proclisis contexts are the hard ones | Grohmann 2011 and the CAT studies treat placement variation as bilectal pressure from SMG rather than as a stage, with social predictors | For adult learners both agree on the practical point: proclisis contexts (complementizers, negation, wh) are where errors concentrate. Weight those contexts higher in drills and in error anticipation |
| Status of the koine: continuum or two lects (sections 1, 7, 8) | Tsiplakou 2014, Kappler and Tsiplakou 2018 and Tsiplakou et al. 2006 describe a continuum with a mesolectal koine (the draft's one-verb register ladder επιορώννασιν, επιερώννασιν, πλήρωνναν, πλήρωναν from the Πολίτης column was not confirmed and is flagged in section 7) that mixes palatals, proclisis and periphrastic tenses with SMG forms. Arvaniti 2010 adds a third pole, "Cypriot Standard Greek", distinct from SMG | Rowe and Grohmann 2013 reject the continuum and argue for discrete bilectalism (CG as L, SMG as H) with co-overt prestige. Sophocleous and Wilks 2010 find kindergarten children already separating the two varieties by interlocutor | Model two registers (koine CG and SMG) as the interface the learner sees, and implement the continuum underneath as a feature ladder (basilectal, koine, acrolectal) on individual items. Teach the koine, label basilectal forms as regional (χωρκάτικα in the sources' own terms), label SMG forms as standard |
| Origin and stability of the koine (sections 1, 7) | Terkourafi 2005 traces the generalised variety to koineisation with roots in the 14th century | Tsiplakou et al. 2006 and Tsiplakou 2014 describe recent levelling and partial convergence to SMG, mainly in morphology and lexis, with the koine's prestige buffering further loss | No design consequence beyond register notes. Use Tsiplakou 2014 and Tsiplakou and Armostis 2020 to decide which local features count as koine (taught) and which as regional (shown, not drilled) |
| Standard to measure against (sections 1, 7) | Most sources use SMG as the H variety | Arvaniti 2010 (Mediterranean Language Review) argues that the standard in Cyprus is Cypriot Standard Greek, with its own phonetics | Treat "standard" in all feedback as the Cyprus standard, not Athenian SMG, for pronunciation. Use SMG for morphology and syntax contrasts, where the sources agree |
| Spelling of the dialect (section 6) | The Συντυχιές convention (Armosti et al. 2014, Themistocleous et al. 2012): geminates written in every word, caron on σ, ζ, ξ, ψ for postalveolars, final ν kept, synizesis written by environment (Αρμοστής et al. 2016) | Every open data source writes plain Greek letters or Roman script: Common Voice el-CY, the hb20007 corpus, Greeklish chat (Themistocleous 2010), Wiktionary with its own conventions, Wikipedia with combining caron, Wikisource with historical digraphs (τζι, σι), Papadima et al. (ΤΕΠΑΚ) proposing a separate harmonised system | Store every form in the Συντυχιές convention as the canonical layer, keep a normalisation table to and from plain spelling, accept Greeklish input through a transliterator, and show learners the plain spelling unless they opt into the diacritics. No source states an accent rule for the negator εν versus έν, the only documented device is Αρμοστής 2017's etymological apostrophe (έν' from ένι, section 6), so the house rule must be set by the author |
| Attitudes to basilectal forms (sections 7, 8) | Papapavlou 1998 and Papapavlou and Sophocleous 2009: CG rated lower on status, basilectal features avoided. Tsiplakou's classroom data: pupils call them χωρκάτικα, βαρετά | Ayiomamitou and Yiakoumetti 2017: pupils asked to write CG overproduce basilectal and hyperdialectal forms. Fotiou and Grohmann 2022: Nicosia speakers see their own speech as neutral. Karatsareas 2018: London attitudes to CG more negative than Pavlou and Christodoulou 2001 found in Cyprus | Default to urban koine, present basilectal items as regional and optional, and word feedback neutrally. Diaspora learners (UK) may reject basilectal content outright, so make the regional layer switchable |
| What the ASR data represent (sections 4, 6, 9) | The Stamou et al. 2026 baseline and the Parakeet model card report WER on Common Voice el-CY, 10 speakers of spontaneous speech, transcribed in plain spelling | Themistocleous 2017 and 2019 classify Cypriot against Athenian speech from lab recordings of 45 speakers, with vowel duration and sonorant spectra as the discriminating cues | Treat WER figures as a benchmark for recognising learner speech, not as evidence about Cypriot phonology. Pronunciation targets come from the phonetic studies, and the spelling of recognised text has to pass through the normaliser before any comparison with the canonical form |
